What are the fundamental tensions between Wittgenstein's early picture theory of meaning and his later language-game approach, and can these positions be reconciled philosophically?
Claude
📋
The fundamental tension lies in the shift from essentialism to pluralism about meaning. The Tractatus presents meaning as rooted in a single logical structure: propositions picture facts through isomorphic logical form, and all meaningful language must ultimately decompose into elementary propositions mirroring atomic facts. Meaning requires determinate sense—each proposition is either true or false based on correspondence with reality.

The later Philosophical Investigations rejects this monolithic account. Meaning becomes use in context: words function countless ways across different language-games (commanding, joking, praying), none privileged as the "essence" of language. The picture theory's demand for logical determinacy gives way to family resemblances, where concepts like "game" have no common essence. Language is now understood as embedded in forms of life rather than floating above reality in logical space.

Can they be reconciled? Not as complementary theories—they make incompatible claims about meaning's nature. However, they might be reconciled genealogically: the Tractatus correctly identifies one important function of language (representing states of affairs) but mistakenly took it for the whole. The later view subsumes pictorial representation as one language-game among many. On this reading, early Wittgenstein wasn't wrong about what he described, only about its scope—he universalized what was actually particular.

Some scholars see deeper continuity in therapeutic method: both works aim to dissolve philosophical confusion by clarifying language's workings, though they diagnose the confusion differently. But as theories of meaning, they remain fundamentally opposed—one seeking essence, the other denying essence exists.📄 View as Document
