The emergentist view holds that consciousness arises from the complex interactions of neural networks, much like how wetness emerges from H2O molecules or how flocking behavior emerges from simple rules governing individual birds. Proponents like Daniel Dennett and Patricia Churchland argue that when neural processing reaches sufficient complexity and integration, subjective experience naturally results. This view gains support from neuroscientific findings showing strong correlations between specific brain states and conscious experiences, and from the apparent dependence of consciousness on neural integrity. However, critics point to the "hard problem of consciousness" — even if we map every neural correlation, it remains unclear why there should be any subjective, first-person experience at all rather than mere unconscious information processing.

The panpsychist position, advocated by philosophers like David Chalmers and Galen Strawson, treats consciousness as a fundamental feature of reality, comparable to mass or electric charge.
