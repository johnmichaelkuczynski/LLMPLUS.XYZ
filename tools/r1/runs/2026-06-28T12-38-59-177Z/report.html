A Scholarly Inquiry on Constitutional Interpretive Authority


Here is an open-ended question that touches on fundamental issues of constitutional theory and institutional legitimacy:

To what extent can a constitutional system maintain democratic legitimacy when the meaning of its foundational text is determined primarily by an unelected judiciary operating under doctrines of life tenure and counter-majoritarian review?

This question implicates several enduring tensions in constitutional democracies, particularly those operating under written constitutions with judicial review mechanisms similar to the United States model. The inquiry operates at multiple levels simultaneously.

At the theoretical level, it forces us to confront the relationship between popular sovereignty and constitutional constraint. If "we the people" are sovereign, what justifies binding current majorities to interpretations of text authored by long-dead supermajorities? The standard answer invokes precommitment theory—that constitutional provisions represent our better angels speaking to our baser impulses. But this merely relocates rather than resolves the democratic difficulty, because it leaves open who gets to distinguish our better from our baser selves.

At the institutional level, the question highlights the peculiar role of courts in constitutional systems. Judges are selected through political processes but then insulated from political accountability through life tenure and salary protection. They wield what Alexander Bickel termed "the counter-majoritarian difficulty"—the power to invalidate the acts of elected representatives. Yet as Jeremy Waldron has argued, this arrangement treats disagreement about rights as a problem to be solved by judicial fiat rather than as an inevitable feature of free societies that must be worked through democratically.
