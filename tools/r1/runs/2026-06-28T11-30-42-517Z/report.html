A Question on the Nature of Legal Precedent and Institutional Memory


Here is an open-ended scholarly question worth examining:

To what extent does the doctrine of stare decisis function as genuine constraint versus post-hoc rationalization in constitutional adjudication, and what does this tell us about the relationship between law and power?

This question sits at the intersection of legal philosophy, political science, and institutional analysis. On one side, we have the formalist view that precedent operates as a meaningful constraint on judicial discretion—that judges are bound by prior rulings even when they disagree with them, and that this binding force creates predictability and constrains arbitrary power. The Supreme Court regularly invokes stare decisis as a reason for upholding decisions the current majority might not have reached de novo, suggesting the doctrine has real teeth.

On the other side, we have the realist observation that the Supreme Court overturns precedent with some regularity, particularly in constitutional cases where stare decisis is acknowledged to be weaker. Critical scholars note that invocations of precedent seem curiously selective—strong when convenient, weak when inconvenient. The doctrine appears to have an almost chameleon-like quality, firm enough to justify outcomes a court wants to preserve, flexible enough to permit outcomes it wants to reach.

The deeper puzzle concerns what this pattern reveals about law as an institution. If stare decisis functions primarily as constraint, it suggests law operates with some autonomy from raw political preference—that institutional norms and professional commitments create meaningful friction against pure will-to-power. If it functions primarily as rationalization, it suggests law is more epiphenomenal, a legitimating vocabulary draped over decisions reached on other grounds.

The most sophisticated answer likely lies somewhere between these poles, but where exactly? And how would we even know?
