What are the epistemological implications of treating mathematical objects as discovered rather than invented, and how does this relate to the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in physics?
Claude
📋
The discovery versus invention debate in the philosophy of mathematics strikes at fundamental questions about the relationship between mind and reality, and the puzzle of mathematics' physical applicability sharpens this debate considerably.

If mathematical objects are discovered rather than invented, we embrace mathematical Platonism—the view that mathematical entities exist independently of human minds in some abstract realm. On this view, when mathematicians prove theorems about prime numbers or complex manifolds, they uncover truths about entities that would exist even if no conscious beings had ever evolved. The epistemological implications are profound and troubling. How do physical, causally-isolated minds gain knowledge of abstract, acausal objects? This is the classic access problem. We can see trees because photons bounce off them into our eyes, but no analogous causal story explains our knowledge of the number seven or the Mandelbrot set. Platonists must posit some special faculty of mathematical intuition or accept that our best mathematical beliefs are merely reliably formed without being causally connected to their objects—a position that sits uncomfortably with naturalistic epistemology.

The discovery view does, however, offer an elegant explanation for Eugene Wigner's famous puzzle about the "unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences." If mathematical structures exist independently and physical reality is somehow structured mathematically—or even is a mathematical structure, as Max Tegmark's mathematical universe hypothesis suggests—then of course our mathematical discoveries apply to physics. We're discovering the deep grammar of reality itself. The mathematician and the physicist study the same abstract structures from different angles. This explains not just that mathematics works in physics, but that obscure, purely abstract mathematics developed for its own sake (like Riemannian geometry or group theory) later turns out to describe physical phenomena (general relativity and particle physics, respectively). The Platonist can say: mathematicians stumbled upon these structures early because they exist necessarily; physicists found them later because contingent physical reality instantiates them.

The tension between creativity and pattern matching in LLM-based theorem proving cuts to the heart of whether these systems engage in genuine mathematical reasoning or sophisticated statistical mimicry. The fundamental limitations cluster around several interrelated constraints.

The Depth-versus-Breadth Problem in Mathematical Reasoning

LLMs excel at what might be called "horizontal" reasoning—recognizing patterns across vast corpora and applying established techniques to problems that resemble training examples. However, mathematical creativity often demands "vertical" reasoning: the construction of novel conceptual frameworks, the identification of non-obvious lemmas that unlock entire problem classes, or the insight that two apparently unrelated domains share deep structural similarities. These leaps typically emerge not from pattern matching across surface features but from sustained engagement with a problem's internal structure in ways that generate genuinely new mathematical objects.

Consider Gödel's incompleteness theorems or the development of category theory. These advances required mathematicians to step outside existing frameworks and construct new conceptual machinery. An LLM trained on pre-Gödel mathematics would have all the syntactic patterns of formal systems but would lack any mechanism for the metalevel insight that formal systems contain unprovable truths expressible in their own language. The creativity involved wasn't recognizing a pattern but seeing that a certain kind of question could be posed at all.

The Problem of Sparse Justification and Verification

Mathematical proofs carry an unusual epistemological burden: they must be not merely convincing but absolutely rigorous, admitting no ambiguity about validity. LLMs generate plausible-seeming mathematical text, but "plausible" and "valid" diverge sharply in mathematics. An LLM might produce a proof sketch that appears sophisticated—invoking relevant theorems, using appropriate terminology—while containing a subtle flaw that invalidates the entire argument. Unlike natural language tasks where small errors often don't undermine overall utility, a single logical gap renders a proof worthless.
