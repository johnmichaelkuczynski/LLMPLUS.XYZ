The Rule 3241 analysis occupies the complaint's conceptual center because it addresses the upstream conflict that preceded all downstream transactions. Rule 3241 prohibits a registered person from acting as fiduciary for a customer account without the member firm's written approval, and it requires the firm to establish supervisory procedures reasonably designed to ensure compliance. The Durable Financial Power of Attorney, reproduced as Exhibit D, names Violet Louise Reid as successor attorney-in-fact in Section 7. The Trust instrument separately names Violet Louise Reid as backup successor trustee. Violet Louise Reid is employed by UBS Financial Services Inc., as confirmed by LinkedIn profile data, FINRA BrokerCheck records showing employment overlapping the January 2025 transfer date, and the trustee's own admissions in discovery responses identifying Violet as her daughter and acknowledging Violet's UBS employment. The January 1, 2025 transfer of approximately $7 million from Charles Schwab to UBS was executed under the Power of Attorney by Carolina Reid, the attorney-in-fact, who is Violet's mother. The transfer therefore placed $7 million in UBS custody under instruments naming a current UBS employee as successor fiduciary in two separate capacities—successor attorney-in-fact and backup successor trustee.

Rule 3241 exists to prevent exactly this configuration. The rule's supervisory obligations are triggered when a registered person is "acting as" or "associated with the account of a customer in a fiduciary capacity," which includes named successor fiduciaries even if they have not yet assumed active control. The member firm must approve the arrangement in writing, must establish heightened supervisory procedures, and must create and preserve records documenting both the approval and the supervisory framework. UBS has produced none of these records. The complaint does not allege that UBS affirmatively denied approval or that Violet Reid herself recommended the transfer or participated in account management; the complaint alleges that UBS accepted a $7 million transfer under instruments facially triggering Rule 3241 without producing any evidence of the supervisory review that the rule mandates. The absence of a Rule 3241 approval memorandum, conflicts-committee review, firewall protocol, or recusal acknowledgment is the violation. The subsequent liquidation and restructuring occurred in the absence of the supervisory baseline that Rule 3241 exists to establish.

The Rule 3270 outside-business-activities analysis reinforces the Rule 3241 failure. Rule 3270 requires registered persons to provide prior written notice to the member firm before engaging in any business activity outside the scope of their relationship with the firm, and it requires the firm to review the notice for conflicts of interest, customer-harm risk, and supervisory implications. Serving as successor attorney-in-fact or backup successor trustee for a customer account holding $7 million qualifies as an outside business activity under Rule 3270, even if the registered person has not yet assumed active control, because the named fiduciary role creates potential conflicts (duty to customer as beneficiary versus duty to firm as employer, access to confidential account information, incentive to direct assets toward firm products) that the firm must evaluate before permitting the arrangement. UBS has produced no Rule 3270 disclosure from Violet Reid, no supervisory review of such a disclosure, and no approval or denial of the outside activity. The absence of these records suggests that the conflict was never disclosed, reviewed, or approved—a violation of both Rule 3270 (by the registered person, if the disclosure obligation was triggered and not satisfied) and Rule 3110 (by the firm, for failing to establish supervisory procedures reasonably designed to ensure Rule 3270 compliance).

The cluster of red flags enumerated on PDF pages 9-10 of the FINRA complaint aggregates nine discrete facts that, taken together, should have triggered heightened compliance review under any reasonable supervisory system: (1) 87-year-old principal at time of inbound transfer, implicating senior-investor protections; (2) Power of Attorney naming UBS employee as successor, implicating Rule 3241; (3) $6.9 million liquidation generating $473,533.05 avoidable tax liability, implicating suitability and tax-efficiency obligations; (4) one-to-six account fragmentation with no disclosed client-benefit rationale, implicating churning and fee-layering risks; (5) same-day wire of $70,582.79 to trustee's outside litigation counsel, implicating self-dealing and misappropriation risks; (6) fifteen months of zero distributions to any beneficiary despite mandatory-distribution language, implicating breach-of-trust and financial-exploitation risks; (7) sworn Financial Statement disclosing only one of six accounts, implicating fraud-on-the-court and concealment; (8) undated restriction notice issued by firm group rather than named individual, implicating books-and-records deficiencies; (9) multi-forum litigation including federal disgorgement motion, state-court accounting proceeding, and Adult Protective Services investigation, implicating reputational risk and regulatory-referral exposure. The senior-investor protection obligations under Rule 2165 deserve independent analysis because they were triggered at the January 2025 transfer and remained in effect throughout the August liquidation and October restructuring. Rule 2165 requires member firms to establish and maintain procedures reasonably designed to detect and respond to suspected financial exploitation of "specified adults," defined to include customers aged 65 or older. Jane Casey Hughes was 87 years old at the time of the January 2025 Schwab-to-UBS transfer. The transfer was executed by her daughter under a Power of Attorney, not by the principal herself. The transfer moved $7 million out of one custodian and into another custodian where the attorney-in-fact's daughter was employed, without any documented communication with the principal or verification that the principal understood and consented to the transfer. The August 2025 liquidation of $6.9 million in long-held equities, generating $473,533 in avoidable tax and producing zero distributions to beneficiaries, occurred less than eight months after the transfer, while the principal remained 87 years old. The October 2025 restructuring and same-day wire to the trustee's litigation counsel occurred nine months after the transfer. The fifteen-month pattern of zero distributions, culminating in the trustee's January 7, 2026 email stating "I do not expect any distribution in 2026," extended the non-distribution period to at least two years post-transfer, during which the principal aged from 87 to 89 with no benefit from assets ostensibly held for her and her children's benefit. This fact pattern—elderly principal, third-party control, asset transfer to institution employing controller's daughter, large liquidation generating tax harm, restructuring generating fees, wire to controller's counsel, prolonged non-distribution, sworn misstatement concealing account fragmentation—is the paradigm case Rule 2165 exists to detect. UBS has produced no Rule 2165 escalation record, no APS referral, no internal incident log, no supervisory review documenting the decision not to escalate.

The anti-money-laundering implications under FINRA Rule 3310 and Bank Secrecy Act obligations intersect with the senior-investor analysis because financial exploitation of vulnerable adults is a predicate offense for money laundering under 18 U.S.C. § 1956, and because the transaction pattern presents structural red flags that trigger enhanced due diligence under FinCEN guidance. The January 2025 inbound transfer of $7 million, the August 2025 conversion of $6.9 million in equities to cash, the October 2025 fragmentation into six accounts, and the same-day $70,582.79 wire to a law firm collectively present the profile of layering and integration stages in a potential misappropriation scheme: movement of assets into a new custodian to obscure audit trail, conversion of appreciated securities into liquid cash to eliminate identifiable holdings, fragmentation of accounts to obscure total value from regulators or beneficiaries, and extraction of funds under the guise of legitimate professional fees. UBS's AML program, required under Rule 3310, must include customer identification procedures, suspicious activity monitoring, and SAR filing protocols. The FINRA complaint does not allege that UBS filed or should have filed a SAR, because SAR filings and internal SAR-review records are confidential and UBS has produced no evidence that such a review occurred. Instead, the complaint observes that the transaction pattern facially triggers the AML-program review obligations that Rule 3310 requires, and that UBS has produced no records demonstrating compliance with those obligations—no enhanced due diligence memorandum, no explanation for accepting a $7 million transfer under a POA naming a firm employee as successor, no analysis of the liquidation and restructuring sequence in light of AML red flags.

The books-and-records violations under Rule 4511 extend beyond the undated restriction notice to encompass the entire supervisory record gap. Rule 4511(a) requires member firms to make and preserve books and records as required under the Securities Exchange Act and applicable Exchange Act rules, including Rule 17a-3 (records of original entry) and Rule 17a-4 (preservation of records). Rule 17a-3(a)(25) specifically requires records documenting compliance with supervisory procedures, including the identity of the person performing supervisory reviews, the date of review, and the review's findings. UBS should have created and preserved contemporaneous records documenting: (1) the Rule 3241 approval or denial regarding Violet Reid's named-fiduciary status, including the date of decision, identity of decision-maker, factual basis, and supervisory conditions imposed; (2) the Rule 3270 review of Violet Reid's outside-activity disclosure, or documentation of the firm's conclusion that no disclosure was required and why; (3) the suitability and tax-efficiency analysis supporting the August 29 liquidation, including the identity of the advisor who recommended it, the principal who approved it, and the rationale for liquidation rather than in-kind distribution; (4) the supervisory approval for the October 29 one-to-six restructuring, including the client-benefit analysis and fee-disclosure review; (5) the approval for the October 29 wire to Offit Kurman, including verification that the wire served Trust purposes rather than trustee personal litigation expenses; (6) the Rule 2165 senior-investor escalation decision, including the factual assessment and the conclusion that no APS referral was warranted; (7) the AML-program review triggered by the inbound transfer, liquidation, restructuring, and outbound wire; (8) the response to the July 2, 2025 preservation notice, including the decision to proceed with the August 29 liquidation notwithstanding the notice. UBS has produced none of these records. The absence of these records is not merely an evidentiary gap in the complainant's proof; it is itself the violation of Rule 4511, because the rule requires the records to be created and preserved, and their absence demonstrates non-compliance.

The relationship between the FINRA complaint and the SEC whistleblower complaint is complementary rather than duplicative. The SEC complaint targets conduct violations under Regulation Best Interest, emphasizing that Jill Epstein, as the registered representative servicing the Trust account, breached the Care Obligation by recommending or executing the August 29 liquidation without conducting a reasonable diligence inquiry into the tax consequences, the Trust's mandatory-distribution language, or the availability of in-kind distribution as a superior alternative. The SEC complaint further alleges a Conflict of Interest Obligation breach because the liquidation and restructuring served UBS's financial interest (ongoing advisory fees on re-invested proceeds) rather than the beneficiaries' best interest (tax-free in-kind distribution and immediate access to inherited assets). The FINRA complaint does not repeat these Reg BI allegations; instead, it targets the supervisory infrastructure that should have prevented the violations from occurring. Under FINRA Rule 3110, UBS must establish and maintain a supervisory system, including written supervisory procedures, reasonably designed to achieve compliance with applicable securities laws and FINRA rules. The existence of Reg BI violations by a registered representative raises the inference that the firm's supervisory system failed, because a reasonably designed system would have detected and prevented the violations before they occurred. The FINRA complaint's supervisory-failure theory therefore depends on proving the underlying conduct violations that the SEC complaint alleges, but it shifts the liability focus from the individual advisor to the firm's compliance infrastructure.

The evidentiary burden in a FINRA disciplinary proceeding differs from the burden in an SEC enforcement action or a civil damages lawsuit. FINRA operates as a self-regulatory organization with investigative authority over its member firms, and FINRA disciplinary proceedings are initiated by FINRA's Department of Enforcement based on complaints, examinations, or referrals. The complainant in a customer-initiated FINRA complaint does not bear the burden of proving the violation; the complainant's role is to provide sufficient information to warrant FINRA opening a preliminary inquiry, at which point FINRA's investigative staff assumes responsibility for gathering evidence, interviewing witnesses, and determining whether a rule violation occurred. The evidentiary standard is "preponderance of the evidence" in a disciplinary hearing, but the preliminary-inquiry threshold is lower: FINRA will open an inquiry if the complaint presents a "reasonable basis" to believe a violation may have occurred. The FINRA complaint's strategic design reflects this threshold—it does not attempt to prove the violations with trial-ready evidence, but instead presents a fact pattern and document gap sufficient to demonstrate that FINRA cannot determine compliance without conducting an inquiry and compelling UBS to produce its internal supervisory records.

The specific request for FINRA action, enumerated on PDF page 14, identifies the compliance records UBS should produce in response to a preliminary inquiry: (1) complete Rule 3241 approval file, including application, conflicts analysis, supervisory conditions, and annual review records; (2) Rule 3270 outside-activity disclosure from Violet Reid, or firm memorandum documenting conclusion that no disclosure was required; (3) conflicts-committee review of the employee-fiduciary issue, if any such review occurred; (4) incident log or supervisory file documenting internal escalation of the Trust account for heightened review; (5) Rule 2165 senior-investor escalation record, including APS-referral consideration; (6) AML-program review file, including enhanced due diligence memoranda and SAR-consideration records; (7) dated source documents for the restriction notice, including email transmittal metadata, supervisory approval, and factual basis memorandum. The complaint anticipates that UBS will either (a) produce these records, in which case FINRA and the complainant can evaluate whether the supervisory decisions were reasonable and consistent with FINRA rules, or (b) admit that the records do not exist, in which case the absence itself establishes the Rule 4511 violation and raises the inference that the underlying supervisory obligations (Rules 3241, 3270, 3110, 2165, 3310) were not satisfied.

The complaint's rhetorical structure alternates between affirmative factual assertions (the liquidation occurred, the restructuring occurred, the wire occurred) and negative documentary gaps (no Rule 3241 approval exists, no Rule 2165 escalation exists, no dated restriction notice exists). This alternating structure mirrors the evidentiary burden in a supervisory-failure case: the complainant must prove the facts that trigger supervisory obligations, but the firm must produce the supervisory records demonstrating compliance with those obligations. The complainant cannot prove a negative (that no Rule 3241 approval exists) in the evidentiary sense of presenting absence-of-evidence as affirmative proof, but the complainant can establish facts requiring a Rule 3241 approval and then challenge the firm to produce it. If the firm cannot produce it, the burden shifts back to the firm to explain the absence, and the firm's inability to explain constitutes an admission of the Rule 4511 violation (failure to create and preserve the required record) and raises the inference of the underlying substantive violation (failure to comply with Rule 3241's approval requirement).

The temporal relationship between the preservation notice and the liquidation introduces a credibility dimension that extends beyond the supervisory-failure theory. If UBS received the preservation notice on July 2, 2025, the firm had actual notice that litigation was pending, that the Trust accounts were in dispute, that document preservation was legally required, and that any material alteration to account holdings would later be scrutinized for evidence of spoliation, obstruction, or bad faith. The August 29 liquidation occurred fifty-eight days later—sufficient time for the preservation notice to have been routed to the servicing advisor, the branch compliance officer, and the legal department, and sufficient time for supervisory review of whether proceeding with a $6.9 million liquidation was consistent with preservation obligations and litigation exposure. The complaint does not allege that the liquidation violated the preservation notice as a legal matter, because the preservation notice was not a court order and UBS was not legally prohibited from processing customer instructions. Instead, the complaint alleges that the temporal relationship invites the inference that the liquidation was executed without supervisory review of whether it was prudent, consistent with fiduciary obligations, or protective of the firm's litigation interests. The absence of any supervisory record documenting that review is the violation.

The October 29, 2025 same-day wire to Offit Kurman introduces a separate temporal inference. The wire occurred on the same business day that five new accounts were opened and the Trust's assets were fragmented across six accounts for the first time. The simultaneity invites the inference that the restructuring was designed to obscure the total asset value from beneficiaries, regulators, or courts, and that the wire was timed to occur during the confusion of the restructuring to minimize the likelihood of detection. The complaint does not rest on this inference as proof of intent, but presents it as a red flag requiring supervisory inquiry: who approved the wire, what verification was performed to ensure the wire served Trust purposes rather than trustee personal expenses, and why was the wire processed on the same day as a major account restructuring rather than through ordinary monthly distribution cycles. UBS has produced no supervisory records addressing these questions.

The sworn Financial Statement filed January 21, 2026 in the Maryland guardianship proceeding (Case No. C-15-CV-26-000381) creates a separate fraud-on-the-court issue that, while not itself a FINRA violation, demonstrates the real-world consequences of the account fragmentation and disclosure failures. The Financial Statement, filed under oath, disclosed one UBS account (KU-882) with a reported value of $933,841, omitting the remaining five accounts collectively worth over $6.5 million. The omission rendered the Financial Statement materially false and prevented the guardianship court from assessing the Trust's true asset position when evaluating whether guardianship was necessary or whether Trust assets were being properly managed. The trustee's ability to file a materially incomplete Financial Statement without detection demonstrates that the six-account structure successfully obscured the Trust's total holdings from third-party review, which is precisely the client-harm risk that supervisory review of the restructuring should have identified and prevented.

The complainant's strategic positioning across three forums—FINRA complaint, SEC whistleblower filing, and Maryland civil litigation—reflects a coordinated approach to leveraging each forum's distinct investigative and remedial powers. The FINRA complaint seeks production of supervisory records and potential disciplinary sanctions against UBS. The SEC whistleblower filing seeks monetary sanctions under Reg BI and potential individual liability for Jill Epstein. The Maryland litigation (now settled as to the civil action, but with guardianship and accounting proceedings ongoing) seeks beneficiary distributions, trustee removal, and asset recovery. The three forums do not duplicate each other; they address different legal theories (supervisory failure, conduct violation, fiduciary breach), different defendants (UBS as firm, Jill Epstein as advisor, Carolina Reid as trustee), and different remedies (regulatory sanctions, monetary penalties, equitable relief). The coordinated filing strategy maximizes pressure on each defendant to produce documents and explanations that may incriminate them in other forums, and it prevents any single defendant from controlling the narrative by withholding information that other forums might compel.

The undated restriction notice's forensic characteristics merit detailed examination because they exemplify the books-and-records deficiencies that pervade UBS's handling of this matter. The notice bears reference number 43772714-2025-1115, which contains what appears to be a date stamp (2025-1115, possibly November 15, 2025), but the letter itself contains no date field. The signature block reads only "UBS AG, Subpoenas, Levies and Garnishments Group"—no individual name, no title, no employee identification number. The letter is formatted on UBS letterhead but lacks the metadata fields that ordinary UBS correspondence includes (sender name, sender email, recipient email, transmission timestamp). The April 8, 2026 correspondence from Elizabeth Small confirms that the letter was "emailed to carolinakreid@me.com on January 6, 2026, in PDF form, with no date on the letter," establishing that the undated state is not a scanning artifact but reflects how the letter was transmitted. If the letter was created on November 15, 2025 (as the reference number suggests) but not transmitted until January 6, 2026 (as Small's letter states), the 52-day gap raises the question of why the letter was withheld for nearly two months before transmission. If the letter was created in January 2026 and the November reference number was retroactively applied, the backdating constitutes a books-and-records violation. If the letter was never created as a contemporaneous supervisory decision but instead was generated in response to the complainant's demands for an unfreeze order, the fabrication constitutes fraud. UBS has produced no metadata, no email transmission record, no supervisory approval memorandum, and no explanation resolving the ambiguity.

The FINRA complaint's conclusion does not request a specific monetary penalty or specify what disciplinary sanctions should be imposed, because those determinations are reserved to FINRA's enforcement discretion and, if a disciplinary proceeding is initiated, to the hearing panel's judgment. Instead, the complaint requests that FINRA open a preliminary inquiry, compel UBS to produce its complete internal supervisory file regarding the Trust accounts, and evaluate whether the facts warrant a formal investigation or disciplinary action. This request is procedurally appropriate for a customer-initiated complaint and strategically sound because it places the burden on FINRA to determine whether the facts warrant further action, rather than requiring the complainant to prove the violation at the complaint stage. If FINRA opens an inquiry and UBS cannot produce the supervisory records, FINRA's enforcement staff will face a binary choice: either accept UBS's representation that the records do not exist (establishing the Rule 4511 violation) or infer that UBS is withholding responsive documents (establishing obstruction and warranting escalated investigation).

The broader implications of this case for FINRA's supervisory-enforcement program are significant because the fact pattern exemplifies systemic gaps that member firms often exploit to avoid meaningful compliance with named-fiduciary conflict rules. Rule 3241 has existed in its current form since 2014, yet FINRA enforcement actions for Rule 3241 violations remain rare, suggesting either that registered persons rarely serve as customer fiduciaries (implausible given the frequency of family wealth management and intergenerational trust administration) or that member firms do not consistently enforce the rule's disclosure and approval requirements. This case presents an opportunity for FINRA to issue guidance clarifying that named successor fiduciaries trigger Rule 3241 even before they assume active control, that member firms must create and preserve contemporaneous approval records rather than reconstructing them post-hoc in response to complaints, and that the absence of required supervisory records is itself a violation regardless of whether the underlying fiduciary arrangement caused quantifiable customer harm.

The intersection of senior-investor protection, AML obligations, and fiduciary-conflict supervision in a single case also highlights the need for integrated compliance review rather than siloed departmental oversight. The January 2025 inbound transfer should have triggered review by (at minimum) the new-account team (customer identification and account opening), the compliance department (Rule 3241 employee-fiduciary conflict), the AML team (large transfer from external custodian under POA), and the senior-investor protection coordinator (87-year-old principal, third-party control). The August 2025 liquidation should have triggered review by the servicing advisor's supervisor (suitability and best interest), the tax department (capital gains consequence of liquidation versus in-kind distribution), and the legal department (preservation notice compliance). The October 2025 restructuring and same-day wire should have triggered review by the fee-disclosure team (six accounts versus one, fee layering), the compliance department (purpose of restructuring), and the AML team (large wire to law firm). The absence of any documented review by any of these teams suggests that UBS's supervisory system lacks the integration and escalation pathways necessary to detect composite red-flag patterns that no single department would recognize in isolation.

The FINRA complaint's forensic methodology—comparing three monthly statements to demonstrate portfolio transformation, calculating tax liability to quantify avoidable harm, tracking wire transfers to demonstrate fee extraction, analyzing document metadata to expose fabrication—provides a template for other beneficiaries, investors, or whistleblowers seeking to challenge member-firm supervisory failures in complex fiduciary contexts. The methodology does not require expert witnesses, proprietary data, or insider access; it requires only publicly available account statements, court filings, and correspondence, combined with careful attention to temporal relationships, numerical inconsistencies, and documentary gaps. The second level is temporal: the conflict existed at the moment of the January 2025 transfer, before any problematic transaction occurred, which means Rule 3241 review should have been triggered by the account opening itself rather than by subsequent activity.
